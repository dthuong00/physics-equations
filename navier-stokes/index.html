Pour honey and it falls in a smooth, thin stream. Light a candle and the smoke rises straight for a few centimetres, then breaks into curls. Both follow the same equation: F = ma for something that has no shape of its own. Navier wrote it down in 1822 and Stokes in 1845. It is used to forecast the weather, to design every aircraft, and to follow the blood in your arm. For ninety years it also hid a question nobody could answer: can a smooth flow tear itself apart? In September 2026 OpenAI claimed its AI had answered it. The claim is bigger than the proof: the proof covers an easier version of the question, and the version most people mean is still open.

01

Why can't you just write F = ma for a river?

You know F = ma: one object, one position, one velocity, and a force tells you how the velocity changes. Now try it on the water in a river. Which object? A glass of water holds about 10²⁵ molecules, and each one is hit about a billion times a second. You cannot follow them all - and you do not want to. Nobody asks where one molecule went. They ask how fast the water is moving here.

Stop following. Stand still and measure.

A weather station does not chase the air. It stays in one place and records whatever wind passes. Do the same with water: pick a point, and write down the speed and direction of whatever water is there right now. Do it at every point and you have a velocity field - an arrow at every place, changing with time. Write it u(x, t): the velocity of the water at place x at time t. This is what the equation is about. Not a particle: a field.

Then the equation is F = ma at every point at once

Cut the river into tiny boxes. Each box is so small that all the water in it moves at nearly the same velocity. Every box is a small object, and Newton's law works for each one: its mass times its acceleration equals the push it gets from the boxes around it. One F = ma per box, and endless boxes. That is all a partial differential equation is. It looks harder than F = ma, but it says the same thing.

So the plan is set: F = ma for a small box of water, written with the field u(x, t). You need two things: the box's acceleration, and the forces on it. The acceleration sounds like the easy half. It is where the trouble starts.
02

How does water speed up where nothing is changing?

The river narrows, and the flow is steady: it looks exactly the same now as it did an hour ago. Steady sounds like nothing is speeding up - yet the water at the narrow mark moves three times faster than at the wide mark. A leaf goes wherever the water goes, so watching the leaf is watching one small box of water. A woman on a bridge watches only the spot right below her; a boy runs along the river, keeping up with the leaf. Ask them both: is the water speeding up? Watch the dashed copies the leaf leaves behind, one every second: the gaps between them keep growing. Then watch what happens as the leaf passes under the bridge.

ρ (rho) is the density - the mass of one cubic metre of water. ∂u∕∂t is what the bridge sees: how much the speed at one fixed spot changes in a second - zero everywhere in a steady river. The bracket is the acceleration of the box under the leaf: the change at the spot plus the change from moving. Here the first part is zero, so the leaf's whole gain comes from the second. Physicists write the bracket as Du∕Dt for short.
This slide has measured the acceleration, but it has not explained it. A box of water has no engine: if it speeds up, something must be pushing it. That something is the right-hand side of F = ma. So: what actually pushes a box of water?
03

What actually pushes a box of water?

A box of water deep inside a river touches nothing but other water. Water cannot grab, and it cannot kick. Yet the box under the leaf gained 0.4 m/s every second, so something must be pushing on its surface. What can water do to water? Try the two experiments below: squeeze the box harder on one side than the other, and slide it faster or slower than the water above and below it. Watch when the push appears, and when it vanishes.

Two of these terms fight. (u·∇)u carries swirls along and makes them sharper; μ∇²u smooths every difference away. Everything a fluid does depends on which one is winning. That is why honey and smoke, following one line, look nothing alike.
04

Why does honey pour smoothly while smoke curls?

Same equation, opposite behaviour. Honey pours in a smooth stream; smoke rises straight, then curls. The difference is which of the two fighting terms is bigger - carrying, (u·∇)u, or friction, μ∇²u - and one number tells you.

06

The Millennium Prize Problem

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute named seven unsolved problems, the Millennium Prize Problems, a million dollars each. One is about this equation. It does not ask you to solve it; it asks one question: starting from any smooth flow in three dimensions, does the flow stay smooth for ever?

Why it must be proved

The equation runs every weather forecast, aircraft design and blood-flow model, yet nobody knows whether its answers always exist and stay smooth. If a smooth flow can blow up, the equation stops describing water at that moment, and every use of it rests on a model with a hole in it.

What solving it gives

A yes means the equation is a complete description of a fluid and its simulations can be trusted in principle. A no marks exactly where the model fails and where new physics must enter. Either way, the tools built for the proof are the tools turbulence has been waiting for.

Why it is hard. The other outcome is a blow-up: in a limited time the flow squeezes its swirl into one point, with infinite speed and spin. In two dimensions that never happens, and it is proved. In three dimensions a swirl can stretch along its own axis and spin faster as it thins, and nobody has proved that friction always wins that race.

The prize sheet accepts the question in four forms. The water either fills all of space or sits in a box that repeats in every direction. And you may prove either that every smooth start stays smooth with no outside push, or that some smooth start blows up when a smooth outside push is allowed. Proving any one of the four wins.

What was claimed

Version (C): a swirl in all of space that tightens and spins ever faster, while a smooth outside push helps it along. If the proof holds, that counts for the prize.

What it does not settle

(A) says water left alone never blows up. (C) says water with the right push can. Both can be true at once, so (C) says nothing about (A).

Why the push matters

A push feeds energy to the right place at the right moment. Water left alone only loses energy to friction.

So "AI solved Navier–Stokes" means a proof of version (C), the form with a helping push. The no-push forms (A) and (B) are still open. Still, a blow-up of any kind was thought impossible ten years ago. How can 3D water do what your 2D screen never did?

How can 3D water blow up - and does the water care?

On your screen, however messy the flow got, every speed stayed limited. That is a theorem in two dimensions. In three dimensions there is one extra move a swirl can make, and it is the whole difference.

In two dimensions a swirl can only be carried and smoothed. In three it can be pulled longer: like a skater pulling in her arms, it spins faster as it thins, and a thinner, faster tube stretches harder still. Friction fights this, and the fight gets harder as the tube gets thinner.

The Clay Mathematics Institute asks for the proof to be published and to hold up for two years before it pays the money. Mathematicians are still reading it. In the same week, Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster reported related results, with an argument over who should get the credit.
Did the AI do it alone? No.

Humans chose which of the four versions to attack. The proof builds on a method from two Spanish mathematicians, Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa - "the heroes of the story", said Charles Fefferman, who wrote the prize question. And Lean, the checker that told the AI "wrong" thousands of times, was built by people. What moved to the machine is building the proof itself. That step had never moved before.

88 hours is the winning run

That is how long the run that worked took. It does not say how many runs failed first, or how long people spent setting the system up. A proof checked by Lean is correct however many tries it took. How capable the AI is depends on those hidden tries, and they have not been published.

Does the sea care? No.

Blow-up is a fact about the model. Long before any real swirl reached infinite speed, it would shrink to the size of a molecule. There, "a box of water" means nothing any more, and the equation was never valid to begin with. The result marks the edge of the map, not a new thing water can do.

So the equation is F = ma for water. It works everywhere it has ever been tested. And, at least when something is stirring it, it carries a built-in breaking point that real water never reaches. The law you started with has now been to the sky with Newton and into the water with Navier and Stokes.
