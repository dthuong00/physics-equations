In 1964 John Bell found a number that no world of pre-existing, locally decided facts can push past 2. Quantum mechanics predicts 2√2 ≈ 2.83 - and every experiment since agrees with quantum mechanics. Walk through the argument, then run the experiment yourself.

01

Two ways to explain a perfect correlation

Send two photons from one source to two distant labs. Measure both the same way, and the results always match. Everyone agrees on the data - the fight is over why they match.

EINSTEIN'S VIEW · HIDDEN VARIABLES

"They agreed on answers at the source."

Like a pair of gloves shipped in two boxes: open yours and find a left glove, and you instantly know the far box holds the right one. No magic - the information travelled inside the boxes all along. Each photon carries a hidden instruction list λ, written at the source; measurement just reads it out.

vs
QUANTUM VIEW · ENTANGLEMENT

"There are no answers until you look."

The pair is one shared state - neither photon has a polarization of its own. A measurement is the moment an answer comes into existence, for both photons at once, however far apart they are. Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance" and refused to accept it.

For thirty years this stayed philosophy, not physics: both views predicted identical results for every experiment anyone could imagine. In 1964 John Bell found the loophole - a measurable number the two views cannot agree on. The rest of this lesson builds that number.
02

One source, two far-away stations

A source produces photon pairs and sends one photon to Alice, one to Bob. Each station holds a polarizer that can be set to one of two angles, with a detector behind each of its two exits. Every photon comes out of exactly one exit - that is the measurement, and its result is written as +1 or −1.

What +1 and −1 mean

The polarizer splits light between two exits. Leaving through the exit aligned with the filter's axis clicks the green detector: +1. Leaving through the perpendicular exit clicks the orange one: −1. Never both, never neither.

Two settings each

Alice measures at angle a or a′, Bob at b or b′. Each picks one, freshly and at random, for every single pair - while the photons are already in flight.

Too far to whisper

Because the settings are chosen in flight, no signal - not even at light speed - can carry Alice's choice to Bob's photon in time. Each photon answers on its own.

Why two angles per station? Ask each side only one question and Einstein wins automatically - a glove-style instruction list can mimic any single-question statistics, even perfect matching. The trap needs each photon to be prepared for two incompatible questions while only one is ever asked. Two settings per side make four possible question pairs - and one pre-written list must cover all four at once. That overconstraint is where the limit comes from.
03

What a common-sense world would promise

Spell the glove picture out carefully and Einstein's side rests on two assumptions, each almost too obvious to state. Together they are called local realism - and together they are exactly what Bell put on trial.

REALISM

Answers exist before anyone asks

Each photon leaves the source already carrying an answer for every question it could be asked - a hidden instruction list, usually written λ. Measurement just reads the list.

and
LOCALITY

No faster-than-light updates

What happens at Alice's station depends only on her setting and her photon. Which knob Bob turns, far away and at the same moment, cannot change her outcome.

03

Compress the whole experiment into one number

First, a score for how much two stations agree. The correlation E(a, b) is the average of the product A·B over many pairs measured with settings a and b.

Correlation between the two stationsE(a, b) = ⟨A · B⟩ = P(same) − P(different)
E = +1

The outcomes always match: both +1 or both −1, every single pair.

E = 0

No relation at all - like comparing two independent coin flips.

E = −1

The outcomes always disagree, every single pair.

The CHSH combination - four correlations, one with a minus signS = E(a, b) − E(a, b′) + E(a′, b) + E(a′, b′)
Three of the four setting pairs are asked to agree, one is asked to disagree. A world of pre-written answers cannot win all four bets at once - that tension is the entire trick.
04

Two lines of arithmetic cap S at 2

Take one pair. By realism its four answers A, A′, B, B′ all exist, each equal to ±1; by locality none of them cares which distant setting is used. Score this single pair on all four bets at once:

Per-pair scores = AB − AB′ + A′B + A′B′
Factor its = A(B − B′) + A′(B + B′)
BB′B − B′B + B′s

Whatever B and B′ are, one bracket vanishes and the other is ±2. Multiplying by A or A′ (also ±1) keeps it at s = ±2, always.

Average over many pairsS = ⟨s⟩  ⟹  −2 ≤ S ≤ +2
An average of numbers that are each ±2 can never leave [−2, +2]. No physics was used - only the assumption that the four answers exist per pair and ignore the far-away setting. Any theory built that way, however λ is distributed, is stuck at |S| ≤ 2.
05

Entangled photons break the cap

For polarization-entangled photons in the state Φ⁺, quantum mechanics predicts the correlation from the angle gap alone: E(a, b) = cos 2(a − b). Now pick the four angles so the three "agree" bets sit at a gentle gap and the one "disagree" bet at a wide one.

Fan the four settings 22.5° apart. Every "plus" pair is separated by 22.5°, while the "minus" pair a↔b′ is 67.5° apart.

E(a, b)cos 45° = +0.71
−E(a, b′)−cos 135° = +0.71
E(a′, b)cos 45° = +0.71
E(a′, b′)cos 45° = +0.71
Add them upS = 4 × 1/√2 = 2√2 ≈ 2.83

Past the classical cap of 2 - and exactly at the quantum ceiling (the Tsirelson bound). Quantum mechanics does not just break the rule, it breaks it by the largest amount it allows itself.
