Quantum entanglement · Bell's inequality

Does a particle know the answer before you ask the question?

|S| ≤ 2- the hard ceiling for any universe that writes its answers in advance. Ours scores 2.83.

Einstein was sure that quantum particles secretly carry their measurement results with them, the way a sealed letter carries its contents before anyone opens it. Bohr said no - the result does not exist until you look. For thirty years this sounded like philosophy, untestable even in principle. Then in 1964 John Bell noticed something everyone had missed: the two pictures disagree about a number. This lesson walks there slowly - what entanglement actually is, what each side claimed, the small piece of math that springs the trap - and ends with a lab where you run the experiment in both universes.

01

First, the ingredients: photons, filters, and a strange pair

The experiment at the heart of this story needs only two things: light, and polarizing filters - the same stuff as good sunglasses. Everything deep later on hangs on this one humble measurement, so let's meet it properly.

The measurement: will this photon pass a filter?

Light is a wave that wiggles in a direction across its travel - its polarization. A polarizing filter is a gate with a preferred angle: light aligned with the angle sails through, light across it is stopped. But light arrives in indivisible photons, so each photon must answer whole: pass (+1) or blocked (−1). One filter angle = one yes/no question put to one photon.

The strange part: entangled pairs

Certain crystals absorb one laser photon and emit two lower-energy photons flying apart. Test either photon alone, at any angle you like: a perfect coin flip, 50/50, no pattern at all. But set both filters to the same angle and compare notebooks afterwards: the answers always match - both passed, or both blocked. Every single time, no matter how far apart the two labs are.

Three facts any explanation must survivememorize these - the whole argument rests on them
1Alone, pure noise. Each photon by itself answers 50/50 at every filter angle. No experiment on one photon shows anything special. 2Together, perfect agreement. Same angle on both sides → the two answers agree 100% of the time. 3Distance is irrelevant. It holds across a lab bench - and across 1,200 km, photon pairs beamed down from the Micius satellite in 2017.
Careful, though: matching alone is not yet spooky. Two gloves from one pair, boxed and shipped apart, also "always match". The real mystery only appears when the two filters are tilted differently - hold that thought; the tilt is the whole story.
02

Einstein: "The answers were written down in advance"

In 1935 Einstein - with Podolsky and Rosen - looked at those perfect correlations and gave the explanation any detective would: nobody is communicating, the twins simply agreed on their story before they parted. His picture rests on two assumptions so natural you barely notice they are assumptions:

Realism - answers exist before the question

Each photon leaves the crystal carrying, in effect, a filled-in answer sheet: "at 0° I'll pass, at 22.5° pass, at 45° blocked…" - a definite plan for every angle it might meet. Physicists call the hidden sheet a hidden variable and write it λ. We can't read it; the photon can.

Locality - no influence outruns light

Whatever Alice does to her photon in one lab cannot instantly change anything about Bob's photon far away. Influences need time to travel - at most at light speed. This is the beating heart of Einstein's own relativity.

Together: "local realism"

A world with both properties - things have definite features, and influence travels no faster than light - is called locally real. It is the world of Newton, of relativity, of everyday experience. Keep the name in mind: Bell's inequality applies to every such world, with no exceptions.

The glove story - why the matching seems innocentEinstein's explanation of facts 1–3, in one shipment
1Pack one left glove and one right glove into two identical boxes. Shuffle them. Ship one to Paris, one to Tokyo. 2Paris opens theirs: left! At that instant Paris knows - with certainty - that Tokyo's box holds the right glove. 3Nothing spooky happened. No signal flew between the boxes. The correlation was packed at the source. Entangled photons, says Einstein, are exactly like this.

In this picture quantum "randomness" isn't real randomness - it's ignorance. The photon knows perfectly well what it will do; we just can't see λ. Hence Einstein's famous complaint: "God does not play dice."

"Spooky action at a distance"

Einstein's mocking name (spukhafte Fernwirkung) for the only alternative he could see: that measuring one photon instantly shapes its partner, wherever it is. To him this was obviously absurd - proof that quantum theory was incomplete, a good statistics book missing its final chapter.

Bohr's reply

Niels Bohr insisted the pair is one indivisible system: asking what one photon "really carried" on its own is a question without meaning. Frustratingly, both men matched every experiment anyone could then imagine.

Thirty years of shrugging

Since the two pictures predicted the same numbers for every test people thought of, most physicists filed the debate under philosophy and went to lunch. It took a CERN accelerator theorist working in his spare time - John Bell - to notice that they don't always predict the same numbers.

Einstein's picture explains the perfect same-angle matching without breaking a sweat - that's what makes it so persuasive. To beat it we need a question the pre-written answer sheet cannot fake. Bell found it hiding in the tilted angles.
03

Quantum mechanics: "There is no answer until you ask"

Quantum theory refuses to hand each photon its own answer sheet. Its claim is stranger, so take it in three careful sips:

One description for two photons

The theory writes a single state for the whole pair - "both aligned vertically, plus both aligned horizontally, in superposition." Notice what's missing: any statement about photon 1 by itself. The pair has a definite relationship; neither partner has a polarization of its own.

Measurement creates the outcome

When Alice's photon meets her filter it must decide - and the theory insists the decision happens there and then, genuinely at random, following no hidden script. The instant it decides, the shared state snaps: Bob's photon is now exactly aligned with whatever Alice's photon did.

Correlation, yes - communication, no

Bob cannot feel the snap. His own photon still answers 50/50 at whatever angle he chooses; his data alone is pure noise (fact 1!). The correlation only becomes visible later, when the two notebooks travel - at light speed or slower - to the same table. Relativity survives, if a little uncomfortably.

The one number quantum theory adds - the chance the two filters give the same answer
P(agree)=cos²(α − β)α, β = the two filter angles
Read the formula slowlyit contains everything we've seen - and one new promise
1Same angle: α − β = 0, and cos² 0 = 1 → agree always. ✓ That's the perfect matching from fact 2. 2Crossed at 90°: cos² 90° = 0 → answers always opposite. Also perfectly rigid, just inverted. 3In between - say 22.5° apart - cos² 22.5° ≈ 0.85 → agree 85% of the time. This in-between region is where the two worldviews will finally part ways.
"They carried a plan" and "they decide on the spot" both explain facts 1–3, and both sound equally untestable. Bell's discovery: every conceivable plan leaves an arithmetic fingerprint at the in-between angles - and the cos² rule doesn't have it. Time for the math. Gently.
04

Turning "how much do they agree?" into one number

To compare the two worldviews we need agreement as a number. Score each pair like this: write +1 when a photon passes, −1 when it's blocked, and multiply the two sides. Agreement (+1·+1 or −1·−1) scores +1; disagreement scores −1. The average score over many pairs is called the correlation, E.

A ten-pair warm-upcorrelation is nothing more than an average
1You run 10 photon pairs. On 8 of them the answers match (each scores +1); on 2 they differ (each scores −1). 2E = (8 − 2) ⁄ 10 = +0.6. Positive means "mostly agreeing". That's the whole calculation. 3The scale: E = +1 always agree · E = 0 no relationship at all · E = −1 always disagree.
Quantum theory's prediction, straight from the cos² rule on the last slide
E(α, β)=P(agree) − P(disagree)=cos 2(α − β)
A first crack: the triangle rule

In any plan-world, mismatches obey a triangle rule: tilting by 45° can't produce more mismatches than two 22.5° tilts combined. But quantum: mismatch at 22.5° is 15%, at 45° it's 50% - and 50 > 15 + 15. The cosine's lazy start near 0° is what no answer sheet can quite imitate.

But maybe a cleverer plan…?

Fair objection. One suspicious angle is not a proof, and there are infinitely many possible plans - random ones, correlated ones, conspiracies. Bell's move on the next slide: combine four measurements so that every plan, no matter how clever, is trapped by pure arithmetic.

Keep the picture: the violet cosine bulges past the straight line, by at most 0.71 vs 0.50 at 22.5°. Bell's inequality is the machine that turns this small bulge into an ironclad contradiction. Next: the trap, in four steps you can check by hand.
05

Bell's trap: four questions, one impossible score

Here is the modern (CHSH, 1969) form of Bell's 1964 idea. Alice randomly asks her photon one of two questions - filter at a = 0° or a′ = 45°. Bob randomly asks one of his two - b = 22.5° or b′ = 67.5°. After thousands of pairs they compute the four correlations and combine them into a single score:

The CHSH score - note the single minus sign; it is the trap's spring
S=E(a, b)−E(a, b′)+E(a′, b)+E(a′, b′)
Step 1 suppose Einstein is rightThen every pair carries four pre-written answers: A and A′ for Alice's two possible angles, B and B′ for Bob's - each simply +1 or −1, all four fixed at the source, even though each run only ever asks two of them.
Step 2 one line of algebraFor one answer sheet, look at A·B − A·B′ + A′·B + A′·B′ = A(B − B′) + A′(B + B′). Since B and B′ are each ±1, either B = B′ (then B − B′ = 0 and B + B′ = ±2) or B = −B′ (then B + B′ = 0 and B − B′ = ±2). Either way one bracket vanishes and the other is ±2: every sheet scores exactly +2 or −2.
Step 3 the ceilingS is the average of those per-sheet scores. An average of numbers that are all +2 or −2 must land between −2 and +2. That's it - that is Bell's inequality: |S| ≤ 2 in every local-realist world. No assumption about how the sheets are written: clever, random, correlated - the ceiling holds.
Step 4 quantum's scoreNow quantum mechanics: E = cos 2(tilt). The four tilts are 22.5°, 67.5°, 22.5°, 22.5°, giving E's of +0.71, −0.71, +0.71, +0.71. The minus sign flips the −0.71 into a fourth +0.71: S = 4 × 0.71 = 2√2 ≈ 2.83. Above the ceiling. The two worldviews finally, cleanly disagree.
"But only two questions get asked!"

Right - each pair reveals only one of A/A′ and one of B/B′; the ±2 concerns answers that mostly stay hidden. That's fine: if all four answers exist, the averaging argument holds no matter which two you happen to sample - provided the choices are random and independent of the sheet. That proviso is why real experiments pick the angles at the last nanosecond, while the photons are already in flight.

Why 22.5°, of all angles?

Look back at the chart: 22.5° is where the cosine beats the straight line by the most. The four CHSH settings are rigged so every pair of questions sits at that sweet spot - the one 67.5° tilt contributes E = −0.71, and the minus sign in S turns it into a fourth +0.71.

The ±2 trick is the whole theorem

No wave functions, no philosophy - just the fact that B − B′ and B + B′ can't both be nonzero when B, B′ = ±1. Don't take our word for it: there are only 16 ways to fill in A, A′, B, B′. Check all 16 on a napkin; every one scores ±2.

One line of algebra says S ≤ 2; one line of trigonometry says S = 2.83. They cannot both describe our universe. All that's left is to ask the universe - next slide, and then you'll ask it yourself.
06

Nature's verdict: the ceiling breaks

From tabletop lasers to a satellite in orbit, every honest run of the experiment lands on the quantum side: S comes out above 2, standard deviation after standard deviation, loophole after closed loophole.

What died

Local realism. At least one of Einstein's two common-sense assumptions is false: either particles do not carry pre-set answers (most physicists' pick), or influences ignore the light-speed limit. You may choose which to abandon - but you cannot keep both.

What survived

Relativity - exactly. The correlations cannot carry a message: Bob's raw data stays a perfect coin flip until Alice's notebook arrives by ordinary channels. Spooky correlations, yes; usable faster-than-light signal, no. The peace between quantum theory and relativity is uneasy but unbroken.

What it built

The "useless philosophy" became hardware. Quantum key distribution: an eavesdropper's touch collapses the correlations, and the drop in S exposes them. Plus certified random-number generators, and entanglement as the working currency of quantum computers and networks.

Bell's ±2 argument was airtight, so the universe had to give up the other side: there are no answer sheets. The photon genuinely does not decide until it is asked. Now go watch it decide - the lab is on the next slide.
