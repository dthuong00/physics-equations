F=G M m∕r2- the first law that reached from the ground to the sky

Before 1687 the heavens and the ground obeyed different rules: stones fell, planets wheeled, and nobody thought the two had anything to do with each other. Newton's one line says they are the same act. It weighs the Earth, steers spacecraft, and - once you subtract it from itself - explains why the sea rises twice a day. Walk through it, then raise the tide yourself in 3D.

01

Why doesn't the Moon fall down?

Drop an apple and it falls. Look up and the Moon just hangs there, night after night. If Earth pulls on everything, why isn't the Moon on its way down too? Newton's answer is the strangest sentence in the lesson: it is. The Moon falls toward Earth every second of its life - it simply moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it as quickly as it drops. Falling, and forever missing.

Falling and missing

Fire a cannonball from a mountain. Faster shots land farther, because they have more time to fall before hitting ground. Fire it fast enough - about 8 km/s at Earth's surface - and the ground curves away exactly as fast as the ball falls. It circles the planet forever. That is an orbit: not the absence of falling, but falling with perfect aim.

Then how far does the Moon fall each second?

Here is the test. The Moon is 60 Earth radii away and takes 27.3 days to go round, so from its speed and its path you can work out how far it drops toward Earth's centre each second: 1.4 millimetres. An apple drops 4.9 metres. The ratio is 3600. And 60 × 60 = 3600. Sixty times farther away, the pull is sixty squared times weaker.

That one arithmetic coincidence - 60² = 3600 - is where the r² in the equation comes from. Newton checked it in 1666 and later wrote that he found the numbers "answer pretty nearly". So if the pull weakens with the square of distance, what exactly decides how strong it is to begin with?
02

What sets the strength - and why is G so tiny?

Every pair of masses in the universe pulls on each other. Newton's law says how hard, and it has only three ingredients: how much stuff is on each side, how far apart the two centres are, and one fixed number that converts kilograms and metres into newtons.

Newton's law of universal gravitation, 1687 - the force between any two masses
F=G·M m∕r2the same F acts on both bodies, pointing each at the other
F - the pull, on both

The force each mass feels, in newtons. The apple pulls Earth exactly as hard as Earth pulls the apple - the planet just has so much mass that its answering motion is invisible. There is no "puller" and "pulled": gravity is always mutual.

G · M · m - masses multiply

Double either mass and the pull doubles. G is the exchange rate, the same everywhere in the universe: 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ newtons for two 1-kilogram masses one metre apart. That is about the weight of a single grain of pollen - gravity is by far the weakest force there is.

r² - distance, squared

The distance between the two centres, not the surfaces. Twice as far means a quarter of the pull; ten times as far, a hundredth. It never reaches zero - which is why Newton called it universal: every mass tugs on every other, however far.

Why you feel Earth but not your friend

A friend of 70 kg standing one metre away pulls you with about 5 × 10⁻⁹ N - you would need a hundred million friends to match the weight of a paperclip. Earth wins not because gravity is strong but because there are 6 × 10²⁴ kg of it under your feet.

Cavendish weighed the Earth

In 1798 Henry Cavendish hung lead balls on a fine wire and measured the twist as bigger balls pulled them sideways - the first laboratory value of G. With G known, your own weight gives Earth's mass: M = gR²∕G. A planet, weighed with a thread.

The same law all the way up

Put in the Sun's mass and Earth's distance and out comes a year of 365 days. Put in Jupiter's moons, Halley's comet, a GPS satellite - one formula, one constant, every orbit in the sky. That is what "universal" bought.

Now push the law to its obvious conclusion. The Moon pulls on everything on Earth - including the sea. So the water should heap up on the side facing the Moon: one bulge, pointing at the Moon, sweeping past you once as Earth turns. One high tide a day. But every port on Earth records two.
03

Why does the sea rise twice a day, not once?

High tide comes every 12 hours 25 minutes - twice per turn of the Earth under the Moon. A simple "the Moon pulls the water" can only make one heap. The picture is missing something, and the missing piece is that Earth itself is falling toward the Moon, just as the Moon falls toward Earth. Every rock and drop of water shares that fall. What you can feel is only how much your own pull differs from the pull on Earth's centre.

Three balls, one Moon

Drop three balls in a row toward the Moon. The nearest is pulled hardest, the farthest least. Seen from the middle ball, the near one pulls ahead and the far one lags behind: the row stretches along the line to the Moon. Earth is that row - near-side sea, Earth's centre, far-side sea.

What is left after you subtract the shared fall

Take the Moon's pull at every point and subtract the pull on Earth's centre. On the near side something is left pointing at the Moon. On the far side something is left pointing away - Earth is pulled out from under that water. At the sides the leftover points inward. The sea is stretched into a rugby ball with two bulges, and Earth turns beneath them: two high tides per lunar day.

The tidal pull - Newton's law subtracted from itself, for a body of mass M at distance d, on a planet of radius R
atide=GM∕(d − R)2−GM∕d2≈2GMR∕d3for the Moon: 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ m/s² - one ten-millionth of g
Where the d³ comes fromtwo copies of 1∕r², one subtraction
1Near-side water is a distance d − R from the Moon, so it is pulled with GM∕(d − R)². 2Earth's centre is a distance d away and pulled with GM∕d². Every part of Earth shares this - it is the free fall you cannot feel. 3Subtract. Because R is small next to d (1∕60 for the Moon), the difference collapses to 2GMR∕d³: one power of distance stronger than gravity itself.

Ten-millionth of g sounds like nothing. It moves oceans because it acts on every drop at once, for hours, with nothing pushing sideways to stop the water sliding toward the two bulges. It also stretches the solid Earth by about 30 cm - the ground under you rises and falls twice a day too.

Notice what the subtraction did: the tide falls off as 1∕d³, not 1∕d². That extra power of distance has a consequence nobody would guess: the Sun pulls Earth 180 times harder than the Moon does - so why is the Sun's tide the smaller one?
04

The Sun pulls Earth 180× harder. Why is its tide smaller?

Earth orbits the Sun, not the Moon, for a plain reason: the Sun's pull on Earth is 180 times the Moon's - 27 million times the mass, 389 times the distance, and 27 million ÷ 389² ≈ 180. If tides were about pull, the Sun would own them. But the tide is a difference in pull across Earth's width, and differences fall off as 1∕d³. That third power changes the winner.

The same two numbers, one more power of distanceM∕d² for the pull, M∕d³ for the tide
1The pull. Sun ÷ Moon = 27,000,000 ∕ 389² = 180. The Sun wins by a mile - Earth's orbit is the proof. 2The tide. Sun ÷ Moon = 27,000,000 ∕ 389³ = 0.46. Divide by 389 once more and the Sun drops to less than half. 3Two bulges each. The Moon raises a sea bulge of about 0.54 m from high to low on an ideal water-covered Earth; the Sun about 0.25 m. Both are always there, pointing at their own body.

So the two bulges add or fight. At new and full Moon the Sun and Moon line up and their bulges stack: spring tides, 1 + 0.46. At the quarter Moons they sit at right angles and the Sun's bulge lands on the Moon's low: neap tides, 1 − 0.46. Spring tides are about 2.7× neap - every fortnight, on schedule, from one subtraction.

Half a metre? Fundy gets sixteen

The 0.54 m is the tide on an ideal globe of deep water. Real oceans are basins that slosh, and some are tuned to a 12 h 25 min beat: the Bay of Fundy resonates to 16 m, while the Mediterranean, too small to slosh, manages a few centimetres. The Moon sets the rhythm; the coastline sets the volume.

Earth tides the Moon 20× harder

The same subtraction, from the Moon's side: Earth is 81 times heavier and the Moon smaller, so Earth's tidal grip on the Moon is far stronger. Over billions of years it braked the Moon's spin until one face stays fixed toward us. Every large moon in the solar system is tidally locked the same way.

The day is getting longer

The bulges lag slightly behind the Moon as Earth turns, and that offset drags on Earth's spin: the day lengthens by 2.3 ms per century, and the Moon, receiving the lost spin, recedes 3.8 cm per year - measured by bouncing lasers off mirrors the Apollo crews left behind. Dinosaurs had 23-hour days.

Everything on this slide - two tides a day, springs and neaps, locked moons, lengthening days - came out of F = GMm∕r² written twice and subtracted once. Next: watch it happen. Put the Moon where you like, stand at your own latitude, and read the tide off the sea.

06

Two copies of one formula, and the sea breathes

At the start, an apple fell and the Moon hung in the sky and the two had nothing to do with each other. Here is what the one line has told you since.

Slide 1 · 60² = 3600The Moon falls 3600 times more gently than an apple, from 60 times farther away. Gravity weakens as the square of the distance - and the Moon is not hanging, it is falling and missing.
Slide 2 · F = GMm∕r²Every mass pulls every other, mutually, in proportion to both masses and G. G is tiny; planets are heavy. That is the whole story of why only Earth's pull is noticeable.
Slide 3 · 2GMR∕d³Earth falls toward the Moon as a whole, so only the difference in pull across it counts. Subtract the law from itself and two bulges appear - two tides a day, with an extra power of distance.
Slide 4–5 · M∕d³That extra power is why the Sun, 180× the pull, gives less than half the tide - and why springs and neaps arrive every fortnight. You watched the bulges stay locked to the Moon while Earth turned beneath them.
What Newton would not say

Asked why masses pull, he refused to guess: "I have not been able to discover the cause of those properties of gravity from phenomena, and I frame no hypotheses." The law describes; it does not explain.

The one thing it gets wrong

Mercury's orbit creeps 43 arcseconds per century more than every tug from every planet can account for. Two hundred years of searching for a hidden planet found nothing. The formula was the problem.

And the one thing it forbids

Newton's pull is instantaneous: move the Sun and Earth feels it at once, across eight light-minutes. Nothing else in physics travels faster than light. The law that unified the sky needed a successor.

In 1915 Einstein replaced the pull with geometry: mass tells spacetime how to curve, and falling is just coasting through the curve. It hands back Newton's law wherever gravity is weak - tides included - and pays Mercury's missing 43″ exactly. That is the next lesson.
